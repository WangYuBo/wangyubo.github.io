Let me start with something you probably didn't expect to hear — and might even find a little deflating:

This is not a book about becoming "more disciplined."

Quite the opposite. If you're like me — if you've bought, read, and diligently followed a stack of books on focus and self-discipline over the years, tried the Pomodoro Technique, morning journaling, twenty-one-day habit formation, putting your phone in grayscale — and then found yourself, on the morning of a big report, somehow having just lost an hour to short videos anyway...

If you've closed your laptop and felt that familiar sinking thought: *What did I actually do today?*

Then this book wants to help you set down a burden first:

**This is almost certainly not your willpower's fault.**

What drove me to write this book was exactly that question — the one that nagged at me for years. I used to believe, as firmly as anyone, that focus was a willpower street fight: whoever could endure more, muscle through harder, go toe-to-toe with temptation longer — that person won. It wasn't until I stumbled into a body of research in cognitive science and attention studies that I saw something that made my blood run cold — and, at the same time, let me breathe for the first time.

Your attention keeps getting hijacked not because something is wrong with you, but because you are living inside a world that has been **meticulously designed to steal it**. Every notification. Every red dot. Every barrier-free open-plan office. None of them are neutral. They are aimed directly at your limited beam of attention, and most of the time, they win.

This is not a metaphor. That app on your phone — the one you can't stop scrolling — has an entire team behind it. Engineers and designers pulling six-figure salaries, A/B-testing every pixel's color, every vibration's timing, every push notification's phrasing. Their single KPI is to make you stay one second longer. And you? Your weapon against all of that is five words — "I should really focus now" — and a willpower reserve that's already running on fumes.

This is not a fair fight. **Design is a weapon that runs automatically, around the clock, calibrated to exploit your most basic instincts. Willpower is something you remember to use occasionally — and it tires out fast.** Pitting willpower against design is like bringing a wooden stick to a tank battle. It's not that you lack guts. You just picked the wrong battlefield.

Once you see this, the whole problem flips on its head. If losing your attention is the result of design, then taking it back has to come through **design** too — not through gritting your teeth. This is the fundamental difference between this book and the vast majority of "self-discipline bibles" out there: **We're going to change the layout of the small patch of ground you're standing on, not your personality.** Redesigning your environment is always cheaper — and far more durable — than trying to overhaul your character.

---

The second thing I want to be upfront about is **honesty**.

The field of attention research has had a rough couple of decades. A number of findings that were once wildly popular, cited endlessly, and served up as gospel — well, they didn't hold up under more rigorous replication attempts. (You've probably heard that willpower is like a muscle that gets depleted with use. That ten thousand hours makes you a genius. That just having your phone on the desk makes you dumber. All of these claims are shakier than they sound.) A book about attention that peddles shaky conclusions as ironclad truths — just because they're grabby and sell well — would be doing the very thing it claims to criticize: stealing your attention through dishonest means.

So I set myself a rule: **For every "eye-opening" finding in this book, I'll do my best to tell you how solid it actually is.** If the evidence is settled, I'll say so. If researchers are still arguing about it and the confidence is low, I'll stop and tell you, honestly, using a small box labeled "**Reality Check**" — as in, *don't take this one to the bank*. If something reflects my personal judgment, I'll label it as such. I'd rather you have a few fewer "aha, that explains everything!" moments than sell you something that isn't true. A book that teaches you to guard your attention should, at a minimum, be worthy of the attention you're giving it.

---

Finally, a word on **how to read this book**.

The book follows a simple three-part arc: first, we'll **understand** what your attention actually is — how small it is, how fragile, how easily it gets tugged away. Then we'll **diagnose** why you keep losing hold of it — the illusion of multitasking, products designed to hook you, screens that make you read shallower and shallower, a brain that gets tired. Finally, we'll get to the part about actually **taking it back** — entering depth, redesigning your environment, settling your emotions, and assembling all of this into a system that can actually keep running over the long haul. Diagnosis first, then treatment. Reading straight through works best. But you can absolutely jump around — if a chapter title hits a pain point you're feeling right now, flip straight to it.

At the end of each chapter, I've left you one small action labeled "**Try This**." The entire philosophy of this book is: *a few well-chosen design changes beat a scattered assault of willpower*. If some readers get greedy, get ambitious, try to change ten things at once — well, they've walked right into the very trap this book most wants to help them avoid. When a "Try This" catches your eye and makes you think, *huh, I could actually do that*, stop right there. Pick just that one. Try it for a week.

Taking back your attention was never meant to be a revolution that demands you become a whole new person. It starts with one small, smart design choice — and then another. Reclaiming it inch by inch.

Let's begin.
